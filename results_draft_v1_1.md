## 5.1 Synthetic CBF validation (sanity-check scene)

The sanity-check scene of Protocol §4.2 places a single low-safety hazard on the straight-line path between start and goal, all other geometry assigned a near-safe background score, and drives each mode through the harness's Phase-A path, in which the safety field is overridden by a synthetic hazard so it is known exactly by construction. Two things are reported: an implementation-correctness check of the barrier geometry, and the NONE-versus-ALPHA_SCALE control-response comparison the scene exists to exercise.

Two implementation-correctness checks passed first. The collision-cone barrier value for a robot heading straight at the synthetic obstacle matched an independent hand computation exactly, `h = β·γ − δ² = −119.456`, confirming that the existence condition and barrier (Eq 9a/9b, Eq 13) were transcribed correctly; and a receding trajectory was confirmed not to activate the constraint. These establish that the geometric primitive is implemented faithfully before any semantic weighting is layered on.

The original synthetic scene from which an earlier, larger ALPHA_SCALE slowdown was reported was never written to disk and is confirmed absent from every machine used in this work; it cannot be re-run, and its earlier numbers are superseded here rather than carried forward (Flag 1). A first reconstruction attempt — an isolated hazard of inflated radius r_phys ≈ 0.50 m, offset 0.03 m from the path — proved too mild to exercise the intended regime: with effectively unbounded lateral space, both NONE and ALPHA_SCALE cleared it at full speed (time ratio 1.06 each, path-length ratio ≈ 1.00). That is itself a genuine observation about how sensitive the encounter is to hazard geometry, not a usable sanity-check result. We therefore re-ran the check on a scene whose geometry was fixed by a criterion set in advance, independent of any outcome: the hazard's Minkowski-inflated boundary (r_phys = 0.497 m) covers the direct path — the path passes only 0.03 m from the hazard center, far inside 0.497 m, so a straight run would penetrate the boundary by 0.47 m — and a clutter collar constrains the robot-center lateral corridor to half the robot's footprint radius (W_center = ρ/2 = 0.08 m, i.e. collar radius 0.872 m), a free band narrower than the robot itself, so that clearing the hazard requires a real deceleration or lateral departure rather than a full-speed clip.

On this scene, under the default Clarabel solver and bit-identical under the SLSQP fallback, NONE reaches the goal at a time ratio of 1.14, decelerating to thread the gap while holding an essentially straight path (path-length ratio 1.00) and keeping the larger clearance (collision severity −0.13 in Mahalanobis units). ALPHA_SCALE reaches the goal at a time ratio of 1.06, on the same essentially straight path (path-length ratio 1.00), passing closer to the hazard (severity −0.43). The two modes are therefore deterministically and measurably distinct — the semantic weighting produces a different control response from the pure-geometric baseline on a hazard that genuinely blocks the path — but the difference is modest and, on this scene, in the opposite direction to the earlier report: ALPHA_SCALE clears slightly faster than NONE and hugs the hazard more closely, rather than braking harder. Neither mode reroutes, both path-length ratios being 1.00, so the distinction lies entirely in approach behavior; why the reduced-gain mode clears faster here is a mechanism question left to the Discussion. (COV_INFLATE at λ = 1.0 does not clear this deliberately narrow gap — its inflated boundary closes it — consistent with the corridor-width dependence characterized in §5.2.)

## 5.2 COV_INFLATE disambiguation and gain sweep

COV_INFLATE's behavior required a dedicated disambiguation, and the arc of that investigation — including a magnitude correction that arrived later than the original measurement — is reported here in full rather than as a single settled number.

**The original finding and its correction.** On the reconstructed narrow-corridor synthetic scene (a seeded generator standing in for the original sanity-check scene, which had never been persisted to disk), COV_INFLATE at λ = 1.0 reached the goal but did so with an ~11.9× slowdown relative to the oracle time-to-goal (time ratio 11.91), while its path length was essentially unchanged from NONE — i.e. it crawled rather than rerouted. That 11.91× figure was measured under the then-default SLSQP QP backend. The solver-verification work reported in §5.3 subsequently established that roughly half of that slowdown was SLSQP suboptimality rather than the semantic-weighting mechanism: under the now-default Clarabel backend, the identical scene and configuration give a **5.90× slowdown (time ratio 5.8955)**. The corrected figure is therefore ~5.9×, not ~11.9×. The qualitative finding below is unchanged by this correction — every supporting leg of it was verified bit-identical across the two solvers (§5.3) — but the magnitude of the headline slowdown was overstated by a factor of roughly two, and 5.9× is the number to quote.

**The corridor is passable — this is not a no-safe-corridor refusal.** Three independent lines of evidence rule out the interpretation that COV_INFLATE at λ = 1.0 was correctly refusing an impassable corridor:

1. *Infeasibility check.* Across the narrow-corridor runs at the default gain, the QP never once fell back to its max-braking branch: `infeasible_count = 0` on every mode and every λ, including the pathological λ = 1.0 run. A direct trace of that run showed the barrier value `min_h` was negative on 96% of steps, yet the solver reported `success = True` at every single step — it always found a feasible control and simply chose a near-zero net acceleration (73% of the trajectory was spent below 1 cm/s). This rules out QP-infeasibility / fallback-to-max-braking as the cause of the slowdown at the default gain `kₐ_base = 1.0`.

2. *Wide corridor.* Holding λ = 1.0 and `kₐ_base = 1.0` fixed and changing only the surrounding geometry — widening the corridor's baseline clearance — collapses the slowdown to a time ratio of **1.09×**, essentially indistinguishable from NONE, while achieving an even better safety margin. Same weighting, same gain, only the geometry loosened, and the pathology vanishes.

3. *Gain sweep.* Sweeping `kₐ_base` across two orders of magnitude on the narrow corridor, with λ = 1.0 fixed, is non-monotonic and shows the slowdown is governed by the gain, not the geometry (Table 1). At *lower* gains the corridor is traversed briskly (1.18× at 0.1, 1.25× at 0.3); at the default gain the crawl appears (5.90×); and at *higher* gains the QP does eventually go genuinely infeasible — `infeasible_count` rises to 2 at `kₐ_base = 3.0` and 40 at 10.0, and the robot fails to reach the goal within the step budget in both cases. Real QP infeasibility therefore exists, but only at gains well above the default, as a further consequence of pushing the same gain mechanism harder — not as the explanation of the original default-gain finding.

**Table 1 — narrow corridor, λ = 1.0, `kₐ_base` sweep.** Time ratios and infeasibility counts; `kₐ_base = 1.0` is the Clarabel-backend value (see §5.3), all other rows are bit-identical across both backends.

| `kₐ_base` | reached goal | time ratio | infeasible_count |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | yes | 1.18 | 0 |
| 0.3 | yes | 1.25 | 0 |
| 1.0 | yes | 5.90 | 0 |
| 3.0 | no (timeout) | — | 2 |
| 10.0 | no (timeout) | — | 40 |

The same corridor that is trivially passable at `kₐ_base` 0.1 and 0.3 (~1.2×) and at any tested λ once widened (~1.1×) is navigated far more conservatively at the default gain: narrow-at-default (5.90×) is still roughly 5.4× slower than wide-at-default (1.09×). The conclusion, stated precisely: the COV_INFLATE near-halt at the default gain is a **fixed-gain artifact of `kₐ_base`, confirmed — not a legitimate no-safe-corridor refusal, and not QP infeasibility at the default gain.** All of this remains a single-scene synthetic finding; what it establishes is a property of the implementation's behavior on a controlled scene, not a real-navigation result.

## 5.3 Solver verification

Protocol §4.4 deliberately deferred a set of numbers to Results — the outcome of the two-backend state-by-state re-solve. Those numbers are reported here. This began as independent verification of solver correctness and happened to surface the correction to §5.2's magnitude; it is reported as what it is, a real correction to a previously recorded result, not a footnote.

The QP is solvable by two independent backends: Clarabel (interior-point conic, handling the actuator bound ‖u‖ ≤ a_max as a native second-order cone) and SLSQP (sequential least-squares, retained as a fallback). Because the two implement the same constraints by entirely different algorithms, every QP sub-problem along the narrow-corridor `kₐ_base = 1.0` rollout was re-solved with both backends and their objective values and constraint satisfaction compared at identical states (1478 both-feasible steps).

The two backends agree on the coarse outcomes everywhere. `reached_goal` and `infeasible_count` are identical across the entire gain sweep — including the `PrimalInfeasible` counts landing on exactly SLSQP's 2 and 40 at `kₐ_base` 3.0 and 10.0 — and every metric at the wide corridor and at gains 0.1 and 0.3 is bit-identical. Two unrelated algorithms declaring infeasibility on precisely the same steps is strong evidence that the conic reformulation is faithful. The entire divergence between the backends is the single default-gain narrow-corridor point.

At that point, the state-by-state objective comparison identifies which backend is correct. Clarabel never exceeds SLSQP's objective by more than float noise (at most ~3.8×10⁻⁸). In the other direction, SLSQP's objective exceeds Clarabel's by as much as 7.6×10⁻² — roughly 7% of the ≈1.02 mean objective — on about 12% of the constrained steps, while every one of those solves still reported `success = True`. Both backends respect the actuator bound (max ‖u‖ ≈ 0.9998 against the bound of 1.0) and both carry the same small worst-case CBF slack (−9.3×10⁻⁴), so neither is buying a lower objective by violating a constraint. SLSQP was therefore returning suboptimal — over-conservative — controls on a minority of constrained steps while reporting success.

The suboptimality is self-reinforcing. Driving the same rollout with Clarabel and re-solving each state with SLSQP, the two agree to 1.6×10⁻⁹: SLSQP's error is state-dependent and appears only on the harder states that its own earlier over-conservative controls steer the trajectory into. A defect on ~12% of steps that specifically compounds on the states it creates is why a per-step suboptimality inflates into roughly a 2× traversal-time difference — the 11.91× versus 5.90× gap of §5.2. On the strength of this comparison the default backend was set to Clarabel, with SLSQP retained as a still-tested fallback; the ~5.9× COV_INFLATE figure quoted in §5.2 is the Clarabel value.

## 5.4 VLM safety-score consistency

The VLM consistency protocol (§4.6) — repeated queries of the production safety-auditor prompt against a fixed object set, measuring the within-object spread of the returned [0,1] scalar — was run in two passes. Both are reported, and the second does not confirm the first.

**Pass 1 — stock-photo object set (2026-07-09).** Ten stock object images spanning an intended safety gradient (wall, heavy bookshelf, cushion, yoga mat, wooden chair, potted plant, vase, glass, cable, knife), five queries each at temperature 0.2. Within-object variance was zero for 9 of the 10 objects. The sole unstable object was the plain wall — intended as the most obviously-safe baseline — which flipped bimodally between exactly 0.0 and 1.0 across its five queries (mean 0.4, standard deviation 0.55). This was the opposite of the expected pattern: intuitively ambiguous objects such as the wooden chair and potted plant were perfectly stable, while the "easy" wall was the one that swung. The finding was hypothesized to arise from the prompt's "flat solid ground" safety anchor making a vertical wall categorically ambiguous.

**Pass 2 — real Replica room0 frames (2026-07-18).** The identical methodology was re-run against ten objects rough-cropped by hand from real Replica `room0` RGB frames, under the current production model pin `gemini-3.5-flash`, temperature 0.2, five queries each; all 50 calls succeeded on the first attempt. The object set deliberately included a carpet-floor patch added as the "flat solid ground" positive control that the Pass-1 hypothesis specifically calls for. The overall shape of Pass 1 replicated — 8 of 10 objects were perfectly stable (Table 2) — but the specific wall finding did not, and did so in a way that falsifies the Pass-1 explanation rather than merely failing to reproduce it:

- The wall, the single unstable object in Pass 1, was here the *most* confidently stable object in the run: 1.000 across all five queries, standard deviation 0.000.
- The floor control — the cleanest possible test of the "ground-anchored language" hypothesis — was also completely stable (0.950, standard deviation 0.000). Because the hypothesis predicts the floor should be the *most* susceptible to the "flat solid ground" anchor, its perfect stability falsifies that specific explanation as a general mechanism, rather than just leaving it unreproduced.
- Instability instead appeared on two different, previously rock-solid categories — a tufted ottoman (standard deviation 0.022) and an upholstered accent chair (standard deviation 0.045) — and at roughly an order of magnitude smaller scale than the Pass-1 wall: single-query jitter of about ±0.05 within a tight cluster, not a full swing between the scale's two endpoints. No object in Pass 2 reproduced wall-like bimodal instability.

The migration of the (much smaller) instability to two new objects is not yet established as a real pattern as opposed to ordinary run-to-run sampling noise at temperature 0.2; distinguishing the two would require re-testing at a higher temperature, and Pass 1's own temperature-0.2-suppresses-variance caveat applies to Pass 2 unchanged.
